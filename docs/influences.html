Olfaction, memory and personal meaning

Our starting point is a human question: how can a familiar smell mean something so different to different people? Olfactory research gives that question a scientific history. Cognitive science and neuroscience help us ask how memory, emotion, affect and present circumstances enter the picture.

Selected scientific foundations

  1. Herz and Schooler (2002), odor- and visually evoked autobiographical memories. Primary record. Relevant to emotional experience during recall; not proof of superior historical accuracy.
  2. Willander and Larsson (2006), autobiographical odor memory across the lifespan. Primary record. Relevant to the distribution of memories retrieved under the study conditions.
  3. de Araujo and colleagues (2005), cognitive modulation of olfactory processing. Primary record. Relevant to the influence of interpretation and labels on perception.
  4. Hopfield (1982), neural networks and emergent computational abilities. Primary paper. A computational foundation for associative memory, not a complete model of human autobiographical meaning.
  5. McClelland, McNaughton, and O'Reilly (1995), complementary learning systems. Primary paper. A framework for relating episodic learning and distributed knowledge.
  6. Lee and colleagues (2023), a principal odor map. Primary paper. A useful precedent for learned odor representations; predicting odor qualities is different from learning a particular person's lived significance.

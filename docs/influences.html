SELECTED REFERENCES

Influences & reading

Scent-memory, emotion and affect are foundational to this inquiry. We draw questions from olfactory science, autobiographical memory, cognitive science and AI: how experience acquires personal meaning, and what learning about that meaning could contribute to intelligent systems.

Scent and autobiographical memory

Research on odor-evoked memories gives us a place to ask how a physical encounter relates to personal history. Vividness, accuracy and usefulness remain different questions.

Emotion and affect

We are interested in how an experience feels, what matters to a person, and how that significance changes with context and time. Recognizing an emotion, predicting a choice and acting in someone’s interests are different questions. Scentient’s contribution to each remains to be established.

Association and learning

Associative memory and complementary learning systems offer ways to think about how experience is retained and used. They are intellectual foundations, not proof of a Scentient mechanism.

Human agency

A person’s account can be uncertain or change with time. Our systems should leave room for that, rather than imposing a fixed interpretation.

Voices that shape our questions

EMOTION & LEARNING

“some kind of a value function thing”Ilya Sutskever · Dwarkesh Podcast, November 2025, 13:26 ↗

Asked about a machine-learning analogy for emotions, Ilya offers a possibility and makes clear the analogy is incomplete. We keep returning to the question underneath it: how does feeling shape what we learn and choose?

BIOLOGICAL COMPUTATION

“a biological approximation to a Turing machine”Demis Hassabis, describing the brain · Dædalus, Winter/Spring 2026 ↗

Demis frames the brain as something we can study computationally, while recognizing practical limits. For us, that is a reason to investigate memory and meaning with AI, and let the findings decide what holds up.

These excerpts are intellectual influences, not endorsements of Scentient or evidence for our results.

Selected scientific foundations

  1. Herz and Schooler (2002), odor- and visually evoked autobiographical memories. Primary record. Relevant to emotional experience during recall; not proof of superior historical accuracy.
  2. Willander and Larsson (2006), autobiographical odor memory across the lifespan. Primary record. Relevant to the distribution of memories retrieved under the study conditions.
  3. de Araujo and colleagues (2005), cognitive modulation of olfactory processing. Primary record. Relevant to the influence of interpretation and labels on perception.
  4. Hopfield (1982), neural networks and emergent computational abilities. Primary paper. A computational foundation for associative memory, not a complete model of human autobiographical meaning.
  5. McClelland, McNaughton, and O'Reilly (1995), complementary learning systems. Primary paper. A framework for relating episodic learning and distributed knowledge.
  6. Lee and colleagues (2023), a principal odor map. Primary paper. A useful precedent for learned odor representations; predicting odor qualities is different from learning a particular person's lived significance.

FOUNDING ESSAY

From Sensation to Significance

What lived experience might teach intelligence.

Our foundational inquiry is scent-memory: the connection between scent, autobiographical memory, emotion and affect. We study how sensory encounters become personally meaningful and ask what this can teach intelligent systems that serve people. Human findings, useful computational methods and alignment improvements are distinct results to establish.

We study how sensory experience becomes personal meaning. We build experiences and research environments to ask what this can teach intelligent systems.

A person can recognize a place in a smell before finding the words for it. The same smell can be comforting to one person, unwelcome to another, and different for the same person on a different day. A meaningful memory need not be a preference. A preference need not be a purchase. An intelligent system that collapses these distinctions can sound perceptive while misunderstanding the person in front of it.

Consider an illustrative statement: “That smells like home. I love remembering it, but I would never wear it.” A fragrance recommender might interpret the first sentence as an endorsement. A more faithful system preserves all three statements. A later encounter might confirm the association, qualify it, or change it. The person's account remains evidence about their experience, rather than an obstacle to a model's preferred interpretation.

We begin with scent because it makes those distinctions difficult to ignore. Our ambition extends to the many senses, contexts, actions, and encounters through which people experience the world. We want to understand how those encounters acquire significance, how significance changes, and whether records of that process can help machines make better decisions with people.

This is a scientific question, a product direction, and a company thesis. The links between them must be demonstrated. A compelling experience is not automatically a useful dataset. A useful dataset is not automatically a better learning algorithm. Better prediction is not automatically alignment.

The human question comes first

An encounter can do at least three different things. It can reveal an association someone already has. It can make an existing memory easier to access for a while. Or it can change what the person learns, expects, or values. Those possibilities can look similar in a conversation and lead to very different conclusions.

Our research begins by taking the difference seriously. We are interested in what a person notices, what they remember, what they say it means, and what they later choose. These are related observations, not interchangeable measurements of a single hidden quantity called meaning.

Understanding this process would matter even if it never produced a new AI architecture. The human science has value of its own. The computational question is whether a careful account of experience provides information, representations, or learning problems that existing systems handle poorly.

Why scent

Olfaction offers a concrete place to study relationships among sensation, context, memory, emotion, and action. Research on odor-evoked autobiographical memory reports differences in emotional experience and the ages of memories retrieved under particular conditions. Experiments also show that verbal descriptions can change judgments of the same odor. These findings motivate questions about personal history and context; they do not establish that scent always produces stronger, more accurate, or more beneficial memories.[1–3]

Scent also creates an important encounter with the limits of language. A product name and a chemical description are useful, but neither tells us exactly what a particular person will experience. Even an eloquent account is not a substitute for the physical encounter. Conversely, a physical encounter does not tell us its personal significance without the person's participation.

We therefore treat scent as part of a multimodal episode: a moment involving some combination of perception, setting, language, imagery, sound, movement, and later reflection. Not every episode contains every modality. An imagined scent, a named fragrance, and an actual smelled sample are different kinds of evidence.

Scent is our starting point, not a claim that olfaction is the sole route to intelligence. Its usefulness must survive comparison with ordinary conversation, contextual history, and other ways of learning about people.

Biological computation as a research lens

Brains are biological systems that process information through physical activity and change with experience. Calling the brain a biological computer can be a useful starting lens. It is not a complete explanation of a person, and it does not mean a brain operates like a language model or conventional digital machine.

Computational neuroscience offers ways to ask precise questions about association, retrieval, generalization, and learning at different timescales. Associative-memory models and complementary learning systems are important intellectual foundations for this inquiry.[4–5] They help us reason about what a system must retain, what it should distinguish, and how new experience can change future behavior.

We do not need to copy a brain to learn from these questions. Nor does a resemblance to a neural mechanism establish that an engineering method works. A proposed representation must earn its place against simpler alternatives. A model can be useful without explaining the brain; a theory of the brain can be scientifically important without improving a product.

Our position is to use biology to sharpen questions and experiments, while allowing evidence to determine which computational ideas survive.

From episodes to models of a changing world

A world model is useful when it helps a system reason about how situations evolve and what actions may lead to. Visual realism alone does not establish this ability. An authored scene or generated film can support an experience without being a learned model of its dynamics.

For Scentient, the consequential world includes people. The physical setting matters, but so does the difference between what a system observes, what it infers, and what a person says an experience means. A model that predicts the next sensory event while misunderstanding whether that event is welcome has solved only part of the problem.

Our hypothesis is that well-characterized, longitudinal sensory episodes may provide useful tests of contextual understanding and learning. Can a system distinguish a familiar association from a new preference? Can it anticipate when an earlier observation will cease to apply? Can it use a person's correction without rewriting unrelated parts of their experience? Can it recognize when the evidence does not justify a prediction?

These questions connect personal agents with embodied intelligence. Embodied systems must act under incomplete information and encounter consequences. Their usefulness around people will depend partly on understanding what those consequences mean to the people affected. Transfer from scent-related episodes to other environments is a research ambition, not an established result.

How the research becomes products

Demeter Fragrance Library is our strategic partner. Since its 1996 launch, its approach has connected recognizable everyday scents with personal memories and stories. That makes it a natural starting point for studying scent-memory, emotion and affect. Scentient brings the research and technology. Read about the partnership.

Our human experiences give people a place to explore sensory associations, shape an atmosphere, express a memory, and decide what feels right. Their immediate value must stand on the quality of that experience. Participation in a product should not silently become participation in research.

Demeter Fragrance Library is the first commercial setting for this work. Its fragrances and domain expertise give abstract questions a tangible starting point: discovery online, an encounter with a scent, and a later account of what the person experienced. Demeter is the fragrance business and customer experience. Scentient is developing the sensory intelligence research and technology that can support experiences within and beyond fragrance.

The relationship matters because the research has somewhere real to begin. It does not mean every Demeter interaction is a research observation, every catalog association is a personal memory, or all relevant material is available for unrestricted reuse.

Research can improve these products by exposing distinctions they should preserve: meaning versus liking, recollection versus current intent, a person's words versus a suggested interpretation, and a pleasant experience versus a commercial outcome. Products can, with explicit permission and suitable research design, help reveal whether those distinctions matter outside a scripted demonstration.

The next surface is the environment itself. We are developing environments in which intelligent systems can encounter observations, make choices, and be evaluated against defined outcomes. The Prizm environment work belongs within this program; related person-facing concepts belong with the human experiences. These are distinct development tracks, not evidence of a single integrated system. Their role is to support the mission rather than define its boundaries.

Our intended longer-term offering includes research environments, evaluation resources, and carefully governed multimodal datasets for organizations studying agents, intelligence, and world models. The value would come from meaningful observations and useful tasks, not simply the volume of sensory media. External adoption, reliable human outcomes, and the value of those resources remain to be demonstrated.

What the dataset ambition means

We aim to build a substantial body of permissioned scent-memory episodes connected to human accounts and later observations. The distinctive opportunity is access to lived experience that cannot be recovered reliably from public text alone. Scale is an objective; it is not a claim about the human corpus we have today.

A useful record must preserve what was actually encountered and what was only proposed or imagined. It must also preserve the limits of the evidence. A person's recollection is evidence of their account, not automatic verification of the historical event. A synthetic scenario is a development tool, not an observed life. An expert description is expertise, not a substitute for an individual's response.

We believe the lasting value can come from the combination of domain experience, trusted participation, continuity over time, careful measurement, and products people want to return to. Whether that combination creates a durable advantage is a hypothesis we intend to test. Data becomes valuable through its quality and legitimate uses, not through declarations of exclusivity.

Personal alignment and empathy

Our alignment question is practical: can a system help a person pursue their purposes while remaining responsive to that person's corrections, boundaries, and changing context?

A system might predict what someone will buy and still fail to serve them. It might recognize an emotionally significant memory and still use that information in a way the person did not want. Predictive ability confers neither permission nor authority.

We use empathy as a behavioral research question. Can a system respond appropriately to a person's account, avoid imposing an interpretation, recognize uncertainty, and respect a refusal? We do not claim that a model feels what a person feels. The meaningful test is what the system does and whether the person finds the response useful and faithful.

This puts agency inside the product, not just in its policy. People need meaningful ways to correct, decline, revise, and stop. Those controls are necessary product behavior. Their presence alone does not prove alignment; the question is whether they continue to work in consequential use.

Our commitments

Experience before inference. Preserve the difference between an encounter, a person's account, and a machine's interpretation.

Meaning is contextual. Do not reduce someone to a fixed preference profile or treat a powerful memory as a universal instruction.

Human authority over personal meaning. A system may offer an interpretation. The person can reject it without having to persuade the system that their own experience is valid.

Evidence across time. Immediate delight, persuasive language, and synthetic success are insufficient grounds for claims about lasting human benefit or general intelligence.

Scientific ambition with competing explanations. Use neuroscience and computational models to generate testable questions. Allow ordinary history, language, and simpler methods to win.

What exists and what we intend to establish

Today, our work includes human-facing experience prototypes, memory-authoring interactions, curated domain material, internal research documents, and environment and evaluation prototypes. These establish a body of implementation and a direction for inquiry. They do not establish a large validated human episode dataset, improved empathy, a new biological mechanism, or general transfer to robotics.

Our next scientific task is to determine what the episodes actually tell us. The strongest alternative explanation is that ordinary language and contextual history contain the useful information already. A second is that any benefit comes from additional attention and richer elicitation rather than scent or a special representation. Both deserve serious comparison.

A sensory contribution would be weakened if equally detailed nonsensory records performed just as well. A specialized computational approach would be weakened if simpler methods using the same information matched it. A proposed human benefit would be weakened if it appeared only as immediate vividness, failed to persist, or increased confidence without improving the relevant outcome. Success in a simulation would leave physical and human transfer unresolved.

Our public evidence should make these distinctions legible. Demonstrations show what a system can currently do. Benchmarks report bounded comparisons. Human studies examine human outcomes. Each should be recognizable as what it is.

Why we are building this

Scentient grows from a founder's work at the intersection of Demeter's fragrance business, sensory experience, and intelligent systems. That gives us a concrete domain in which to build, ask questions, and learn from the gap between a description and an encounter. It is a starting advantage, not proof that the scientific thesis is correct.

Our reason for pursuing it is straightforward. Intelligence that participates in human life must do more than produce plausible descriptions of it. It must learn to handle context, consequence, uncertainty, and the fact that significance belongs to someone.

We want to make experiences worth having and research resources worth using. If those experiences can also teach us how intelligence learns from a world that matters to people, the contribution can extend far beyond fragrance.

Selected scientific foundations

  1. Herz and Schooler (2002), odor- and visually evoked autobiographical memories. Primary record. Relevant to emotional experience during recall; not proof of superior historical accuracy.
  2. Willander and Larsson (2006), autobiographical odor memory across the lifespan. Primary record. Relevant to the distribution of memories retrieved under the study conditions.
  3. de Araujo and colleagues (2005), cognitive modulation of olfactory processing. Primary record. Relevant to the influence of interpretation and labels on perception.
  4. Hopfield (1982), neural networks and emergent computational abilities. Primary paper. A computational foundation for associative memory, not a complete model of human autobiographical meaning.
  5. McClelland, McNaughton, and O'Reilly (1995), complementary learning systems. Primary paper. A framework for relating episodic learning and distributed knowledge.
  6. Lee and colleagues (2023), a principal odor map. Primary paper. A useful precedent for learned odor representations; predicting odor qualities is different from learning a particular person's lived significance.
